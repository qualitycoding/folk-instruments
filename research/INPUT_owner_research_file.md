# Acoustic Physics and Construction Variants of Free-Reed and Double-Course Plucked String Instruments for Physical Modeling
Building software synthesizers for the harmonica, accordion, melodion (melodica), mandolin, and bouzouki requires separating these five instruments into two distinct coupled-oscillator architectures: pressure-driven free-reed aerophones and plectrum-excited, double-course plucked string resonators. In the three free-reed instruments, each individual Western reed behaves as a unidirectional, blown-closed cantilever oscillator whose nearly sinusoidal mechanical motion modulates a nonlinear air jet, radiating to the far field predominantly as an acoustic dipole without relying on a tuned pipe resonator. In the mandolin and bouzouki, the characteristic attack, shimmer, and two-stage sustain arise from plectrum excitation of paired strings coupled across a shared two-dimensional bridge admittance matrix into a multi-mode wooden enclosure and Helmholtz air cavity.
Accurate physical modeling also depends on capturing how regional and mechanical construction variants reshape these core equations. Within the free-reed group, unvalved dual-reed chambers enable cooperative vocal-tract pitch bending on a diatonic harmonica, whereas windsaver check valves isolate single reeds and restrict bending on a chromatic harmonica; detuned multi-reed ranks and a wooden *cassotto* chamber sculpt the chorus beating and upper-harmonic rolloff of an accordion; and a shared single air plenum causes polyphonic airflow splitting and moisture-condensation damping in a melodion. Within the plucked group, soundboard stiffness, back-plate coupling, and string-course pairing diverge sharply across carved archtop, oval-hole, and bowl-back mandolins, as well as between octave-paired bowl-back Greek bouzoukis and unison-paired flat-back Irish bouzoukis.
Evidence depth varies unevenly across these five instruments and must guide parameter calibration. Published peer-reviewed modal and aeroacoustic studies directly characterize diatonic harmonica pitch bending, accordion reed aerodynamics and finger/bellows bending, carved and bowl-back mandolin modes, and Greek wooden/carbon-fiber bouzouki soundboard modes. Conversely, accordion *cassotto* spectral filtering figures trace to descriptive encyclopedic summaries rather than peer-reviewed transfer-function measurements, melodica-specific airflow division and reed tapering rely on engineer/builder observations paired with general harmonium free-reed equations, and no peer-reviewed experimental modal frequency table was available for the flat-back Irish bouzouki, whose low-frequency body behavior must be inferred from guitar-like reflex enclosures and luthiery specifications.

### Blown-Closed Instability, Upstream Inertia, and Nonlinear Flow Modulation
Using Fletcher's valve taxonomy, individual Western reeds in harmonicas, accordions, and melodions operate in the (-,+) **blown-closed** (inward-striking) regime, meaning positive upstream pressure (p_1 > p_2) pushes the reed tip toward and through its frame slot. Unlike a simple valve that would remain pinned shut, self-sustained oscillation arises even without coupling to an acoustic pipe. St. Hilaire's theoretical model—corroborated experimentally by Tarnopolsky et al. and Ricot et al.—shows that an upstream inertial effect generates a near-field aerodynamic pressure component in phase with reed velocity, creating net positive power transfer into the reed over each cycle.
Oscillation starts once upstream pressure crosses a threshold p_{\text{th}} governed by the reed's mechanical quality factor Q and any coupled cavity impedance. Experimental comparisons across reeds with Q-factors of 16, 19, 29, 55, and ~250 demonstrate that higher Q-factors substantially reduce threshold pressure. When a (-,+) closing reed couples to a cavity with Helmholtz resonance f_H, Fletcher's linear stability analysis indicates easy low-pressure oscillation when f_{\text{reed}}/f_H > 1, whereas f_{\text{reed}}/f_H < 1 raises the closing-reed threshold above 2 kPa (causing the opposite (+,−) opening-reed configuration to destabilize more easily instead). Because Western reeds behave as blown-closed valves, their sounding pitch settles slightly below their free-air natural resonance, remaining stable across normal moderate pressures while dropping flat under excessive airflow.
Crucially for synthesis, the bright harmonic spectrum of a free reed does **not** come from large mechanical distortion of the tongue itself, but from **nonlinear aerodynamic aperture modulation**. Harmonium LDV experiments confirm that at low blowing pressures (~0.2 kPa, or 200–250 Pa), reed velocity stays nearly sinusoidal, whereas at high pressures (1.4–1.6 kPa) nonlinear flow mechanisms introduce prominent higher harmonics in both the driving pressure and reed motion. In the far field, acoustic radiation is dominated by an **acoustic dipole** generated by strong momentum fluctuations of the jet passing through the reed gap. Moreover, housing the reed inside a **reed cell** (chamber) lowers the playing frequency slightly, acts as an acoustic filter whose dimensions influence upper spectral harmonics more strongly than reed geometry changes, and enhances the second and third transverse reed modes (and potentially higher-Q torsional modes) during transients and steady playing.
## Harmonica Physics and Construction Variants
### Dual-Reed Channel Architecture and Diatonic Vocal-Tract Bending
A harmonica comprises a comb forming individual air channels sandwiched between internal and external reed plates. In each channel of a standard blues/diatonic harmonica, **two reeds are mounted on opposite sides**: the blow reed sits on the inside and the draw reed sits on the outside. Because each individual reed responds unidirectionally, blowing excites the blow reed while air leaks passively through the clearance around the resting draw reed, and drawing reverses those roles.
On a 10-hole Richter-tuned diatonic harmonica, the two reeds sharing one channel are pitched to different notes—for instance, hole 4 on a G harmonica pairs a G4 blow reed with an A4 draw reed. When playing unbent notes, the active reed vibrates near its natural frequency. To access notes missing from the diatonic layout, players use vocal-tract **pitch bending** and **overbending**. Static and real-time MRI imaging by Barrett, Rossing, and colleagues shows that during draw pitch bends, the player elevates the body of the tongue toward the palate while pushing the tongue tip far forward toward the lips and harmonica, altering upper-airway geometry to shift vocal-tract acoustic impedance. As demonstrated by Johnston, Bahnson et al., Millot et al., and Förtsch, standard diatonic bending turns each hole into a coupled **three-oscillator system** (blow reed, draw reed, and vocal-tract air column):
 1. In a draw bend (typically where the draw reed is higher in pitch than the blow reed, as in the lower register), adjusting mouth and throat geometry brings the vocal tract into acoustic resonance with the channel.
 2. As embouchure changes, the initially sounding draw reed slows and drops below its natural frequency while pressure fluctuations excite the opposing lower-pitched blow reed, which behaves effectively as a (+,−) outward-opening reed under draw airflow.
 3. As the bend deepens toward the opposing reed's pitch, the blow reed takes over the dominant oscillation amplitude while the draw reed diminishes, smoothly gliding the sounding frequency down by a semitone or more into the interval between the two reeds.
Beyond downward bends between the two reeds' frequencies, skilled players also use vocal-tract tuning to execute upward-shifted **overbends** (overblows and overdraws) that allow chromatic playing on a diatonic harmonica, a regime analyzed via linear stability modeling and experiments by Johnston, Millot et al., and Förtsch. Comb airtightness strongly affects bend stability and loudness because parasitic leaks between the comb and reed plates bleed air across channels. At the opposite extreme of the instrument, the acoustic resonances of the small chambers housing the highest-pitched reeds can sit close enough to the reed frequencies to interfere with clean oscillation.
### Valved Chromatic Harmonica and Mechanical Artifacts
A chromatic harmonica modifies both pneumatic routing and reed coupling by adding a spring-loaded **mouthpiece slide** and **windsaver check valves**. Depressing the slide redirects breath from a natural reed plate to an offset reed plate tuned one semitone higher. Because chromatic harmonica reeds are often slightly longer and wider than equivalent diatonic reeds, their larger perimeter slots permit significant parasitic air loss if left unsealed. To improve airtightness, makers glue flexible flap valves (windsavers) over the outer surface of the reed slots.
Acoustically, windsavers fundamentally alter bending behavior. Because positive airflow presses the inactive opposing reed's windsaver tightly shut over its slot, the two reeds in a channel can no longer couple cooperatively. Player and builder accounts confirm that while limited downward pitch bending remains possible on a single valved reed (or on half-valved diatonics), chromatic bends cannot match the deep pitch drops of an unvalved diatonic harmonica. A comprehensive chromatic model should also allow synthesis of two physical artifacts documented in manufacturer maintenance guides and player communities: **slide-channel air leak hiss** and **windsaver valve artifacts**, including extraneous buzz when the player's vocal tract excites the valve's own resonant frequency and moisture-induced adhesion ("sticky valves") that blocks or delays reed speech until cleared.

## Accordion Physics and Construction Variants
### Bellows Excitation, Reed Ranks, and Harmonic Bandwidth
An accordion drives rows of riveted metal free reeds mounted on wooden reed blocks using pressurized air supplied by folded bellows. Each reed plate carries **two reed tongues mounted on opposite sides**, paired with **leather or synthetic check valves** that seal the inactive tongue so each key speaks on both bellows push and pull. Unlike Asian bamboo mouth organs (*khaen*, *bawu*), accordion reeds do not rely on tuned pipe resonators: water-channel flow visualizations and wind-chamber studies by Ricot, Caussé, and Misdariis show that accordion reeds self-oscillate purely via local aerodynamic excitation without acoustic cavity coupling. Consequently, bellows pressure serves directly as the primary control parameter governing loudness, attack envelope, and upper-harmonic brightness.
Experimental spectra measured for an A4 note played mezzo forte on an unenclosed 8' accordion register show harmonic partials extending cleanly up to **~10,000 Hz**, decaying in sound pressure level (SPL) with increasing frequency and becoming negligible above 10,000 Hz. According to accordion dealer specifications, a professional 41-key / 120-bass instrument (4 treble ranks, 5 bass ranks) contains 328 treble reed tongues and 120 bass reed tongues—448 individually tuned oscillators—spanning roughly F2 (~87 Hz) or G1 (~49 Hz) on the lowest bass reeds up to A7 (3520 Hz) on the highest piccolo reeds based on informal player measurements.
### Register Layering, Tremolo, and Musette Detuning
Multi-voice accordions combine up to four treble ranks tuned in octaves designated by organ-footage labels: L (16', Bassoon, one octave down), M (8', Clarinet, concert pitch), and H (4', Piccolo, one octave up), routed via mechanical register switches into timbres such as Bandoneon (LM), Oboe (MH), Organ (LH), Harmonium (LMH), Violin (MM), and Musette (MMH). Because each rank sits on separate reed plates and chambers, summing registers in software benefits from modeling slight rank-to-rank variations in reed Q-factor, initial tip offset, and cell geometry, which naturally produce realistic transient asynchrony and spectral differences.
Signature accordion chorus textures rely on intentionally detuning two or three M (8') reed ranks (M-, M, M+). Acoustic interference between detuned harmonic waveforms produces periodic amplitude beating at f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2|. Accordion tuners and dealer reference tables classify styles by beat rate at A4 (440 Hz) and approximate cent offset, ranging from 0 cents dry (*secco*, classical/Balkan), ~2 cents Concert/Violin (~0.5 Hz at A4), ~4 cents Swing (~1 Hz), ~10 cents American/Cajun (~2.5 Hz), ~15 cents Standard German/Italian (~4 Hz, often cited as 440 vs. 444 Hz), ~18 cents Modern French 3-reed musette (e.g., 435, 440, and 445 Hz yielding symmetrical 5 Hz beating), up to ~22–25 cents in Old French or Scottish *Sardo* tunings (6–7 Hz). Note that shop rules of thumb ("roughly 1 Hz = 3.5 cents in the middle of the keyboard") approximate exact logarithmic ratios: at A4 (440 Hz), exactly 15 cents yields 3.83 Hz whereas 444 Hz equals 15.67 cents. Because any constant-cent offset doubles physical beat frequency in Hertz with every rising octave—and conversely, a fixed 1 Hz beat corresponds to ~8 cents at A3 versus ~2 cents at A5—a synthesizer should expose both constant-cent and beat-rate-scaled detuning curves across the keyboard.

### Cassotto Tone Chamber, Open Blocks, and Pitch Bending
Concert-grade accordions enclose one reed block (single *cassotto*) or two blocks—typically the 16' bassoon and unison 8' clarinet ranks (**double cassotto**)—inside a dedicated wooden tone chamber on the treble side. While peer-reviewed transfer-function measurements of *cassotto* chambers were absent from the reviewed sources, descriptive encyclopedic accounts report that the *cassotto* acts as an uneven formant filter that strengthens frequencies between **500 Hz and 1000 Hz** (imparting rounded vowel qualities akin to spoken "o" or "a") while subduing overtones **above 1000 Hz** to yield a warmer, less incisive tone than open-block reeds. Many instruments also include a mechanical mute (*sordina*) switch for additional acoustic attenuation.
Even without a vocal tract, accordionists can execute expressive downward pitch bends on 8' (open or *cassotto*) and 16' registers—though not effectively on 4' piccolo reeds—by tilting a key slightly open over the edge of the keyboard to narrow the air passage through the reed-plate chamber while raising bellows pressure. A peer-reviewed *Acoustics in Practice* (2021) study analyzing the first 20 harmonics during accordion bends documented four quantitative targets for synthesis: finger-only key tilting lowers pitch by roughly **15 cents**, whereas combined bellows-and-finger bending reaches **~25 cents**; all 20 harmonics drop by the exact same cent ratio, preserving strict harmonicity; overall sound pressure level drops simultaneously; and the spectral envelope inverts across harmonic order, with **even harmonics 2, 4, and 6 dropping more in SPL than odd harmonics 1, 3, and 5**, while across **harmonics 7–20 even harmonics suffer less SPL loss than odd harmonics**.
## Melodion and Melodica Physics and Construction Variants
### Breath-Driven Plenum Coupling and Parallel Airflow Splitting
The melodion (Suzuki's brand name for the Hohner melodica, also sold as Pianica by Yamaha) pairs a piano-style or chromatic-button (*Accordina*) keyboard with internal unidirectional blown-closed free reeds. Pressing a key opens a valve so pressurized air from a single shared internal air chamber flows past the corresponding reed and exits through the keyway. Because no dedicated peer-reviewed aeroacoustic monograph characterizes the melodion, synthesis engines must adapt the clamped-bar lumped-mass conservation equations validated on harmonium reeds—which operate across typical chamber/mouth pressures of **0.2–1.5 kPa**—alongside empirical builder and player observations.
Unlike an accordion's large bellows or a harmonica's isolated lip embouchure, the melodion feeds all depressed keys from one compact plenum under finite lung flow. When multiple keys open simultaneously, mass flow divides across parallel airways (A_1 v_1 = \sum A_i v_i in simplified pipe-flow modeling), reducing per-reed airflow and causing different notes in a chord to cross their threshold pressures asynchronously depending on individual reed tolerances. Physics-community experiments on melodica reeds distinguish three airflow regimes: a sub-threshold low-flow regime (silent), a stable moderate-flow regime, and an overblown high-flow regime in which pitch drops noticeably **flat**. Controlled player experiments on the **upstream** mouthpiece vinyl tube also demonstrate that extending the tube up to 15 ft (4.6 m)—even packed with fiber damping—causes only ~13 ms of acoustic propagation delay with virtually no alteration to the player's vibrato waveform, because breath pressure acts quasi-statically on the internal plenum rather than via tube standing waves.
### Vocal Ranges, Enclosure Materials, Reed Tapering, and Moisture Damping
Melodions are manufactured across multiple tessituras and enclosure constructions that directly dictate filter bank tuning and dynamic balance. According to music-retailer classifications, soprano and alto models typically cover roughly **F4–F6** with short, crisp-sounding reeds; tenor models span **C3–C5** for warmer chordal and melodic tone; and bass melodicas reach **F2–F4** with deeper, resonant voices. Standard models employ molded **plastic** bodies yielding a bright, projecting sound, **wooden-bodied** instruments (such as the Suzuki Wood Melodion W-37) use wood casing for warmer resonance comparable to a small harmonium source-filter enclosure, and the **Accordina** mounts melodica reeds inside a **metal** shell with an accordion-style button keyboard.
Internal reed and chamber engineering introduces further register-dependent scaling. Melodion reeds are typically stamped from brass or cold-rolled work-hardened phosphor bronze (PB102), occasionally stainless steel. Craftspeople and reedmakers report tapering reed thickness along the tongue to balance pitch and response: low-pitched reeds are left **thickest at the tip** (adding tip mass to lower frequency without excessive length), mid-range reeds remain relatively uniform, and high-pitched reeds are thinned toward the tip relative to the foot. Because large bass reeds accept more airflow and risk over-excursion or fatigue breakage, melodica builders report shaping the internal air chamber **shallower** under bass reeds than under treble reeds to throttle airflow and equalize playability. Finally, engineering teardowns of stage instruments (e.g., Hammond Pro 44) demonstrate that warm breath moisture rapidly condenses and wicks via capillary action into the hair's-width gap between reed and plate, damping or completely choking oscillation until blown clear—an effect easily modeled as a user-controllable moisture damping parameter on R_r.
## Shared Plucked-String and Double-Course Coupling Physics
### Plectrum Excitation and Stiff-String Dispersion
Both the mandolin and bouzouki use steel or wound-metal strings plucked almost exclusively with a plectrum. Digital waveguide and finite-difference plectrum models (investigated in detail in harpsichord plectrum thesis research by Perng) parameterize plucking transients via **plucking position** (x_p / L along the string, introducing comb-filter zeros at harmonics where \sin(n\pi x_p/L)=0), **plectrum length and stiffness**, and **stroke velocity**. Because a plectrum sweeps across two closely spaced strings in a course during a single downstroke or upstroke, the paired strings receive slightly staggered excitation instants rather than mathematically identical phase triggers.

### Double-Course Coupling, Dual Polarization, and Double-Decay Envelopes
Each individual string supports two transverse polarizations—one normal (vertical) to the soundboard and one parallel (horizontal) to it—coupled through a **2 × 2 complex bridge admittance matrix**. When paired into unison two-string courses (as on all mandolin courses and unison bouzouki courses), both strings terminate on the same bridge saddle and interact dynamically.
Building on Weinreich's (1977) classic analysis of coupled piano strings, Woodhouse (2021, *JASA* 150) formulated the necessary physical conditions for two-stage **double-decay envelopes** across plucked and struck string instruments. Bridge coupling splits identical or near-identical string modes into a **symmetric (in-phase)** mode—which drives the soundboard efficiently, producing a loud initial transient and rapid decay (low Q)—and an **antisymmetric (out-of-phase)** mode (alongside horizontal polarization) whose opposing forces cancel net bridge motion, trapping vibrational energy in a quieter, longer-sustaining tail (high Q). For the fundamental mode of a string to display a strong double decay, Woodhouse showed that the bridge-coupled loss factor \eta_b must greatly exceed the intrinsic air and internal damping loss factor (\eta_b \gg \eta_{\text{air}}). Introducing realistic tuning offsets between the paired strings (evaluated by Woodhouse across ±8 cents) governs the audible texture: at **low modal overlap** (away from strong body resonances), mistuning causes unmistakable amplitude beating ("shimmer") during decay, whereas at **high modal overlap** (near strong body peaks), the system can yield a smooth double decay without audible beating.

## Mandolin Physics and Construction Variants

Benchmark scale lengths run from roughly 330 mm on traditional Italian bowl-backs up to ~352.4 mm on American F-style archtop mandolins. String retailers and players commonly fit light **0.010"–0.034"** sets or medium **0.011"–0.040"** sets (utilizing 0.011" E5 and 0.015" A4 plain steel strings on F-style archtops). ### Carved Archtop, Oval-Hole, and Bowl-Back Constructions
Three principal mandolin body constructions impart distinct modal signatures to the synthesizer's resonator bank. On carved archtop **f-hole mandolins**, twin f-holes flank the bridge without an internal soundpost (leaving top and back coupled only via the ribs and air cavity, unlike a violin). Electronic TV holography measurements by Cohen and Rossing found a primary top-plate vibrational mode at **318 Hz** in an f-hole mandolin; luthier Chladni measurements on carved spruce/hardwood free plates before assembly (mandolin #95) show representative plate modes near 162–164 Hz, 314–332 Hz, and 419–420 Hz, plus an assembled neck-bending resonance akin to the violin B0 mode. Overall, mandolins exhibit weaker plate-to-air coupling than guitars, giving short-scale archtops their fast, percussive decay. Switching to an **oval-hole mandolin** eliminates the f-hole slots beside the bridge, substantially stiffening the soundboard and shifting the corresponding top-plate mode upward from 318 Hz to **423 Hz** in Cohen and Rossing's comparison.
In **traditional Italian bowl-back (Neapolitan) mandolins**, the deep, high-mass, rigid bowl immobilizes the back plate, restricting low-frequency monopole coupling strictly to a **doublet**. Across classical and modern Italian bowl-backs studied via Chladni patterns, bridge admittance, SPL spectra, and finite-element analysis (FEA) by Quintavalla et al. (2022), the lowest peak T(1,1)_1 (linked to the **Helmholtz air-cavity mode**) averages **194.1 Hz** across all instruments (180.3–204.3 Hz; Gabrielli model average **201.1 Hz**), displaying weak bridge admittance yet strong soundhole SPL radiation. The second mode T(1,1)_2 (primary **soundboard monopole mode**) averages **553.6 Hz** (ranging 451.5–694.5 Hz; Gabrielli average **596.6 Hz**) and dominates perceived loudness, followed by the T(1,2) mode averaging **740.5 Hz** across all mandolins (and **697.0 Hz** across Gabrielli models, with individual examples reaching 1035 Hz). Notably, Quintavalla et al. also observed that contemporary mandolins exhibited a much smaller frequency shift when plugging the soundhole than historical examples, and FEA material sweeps showed that soundboard density/stiffness variations can shift T(1,1)_2 by **106.1 Hz (~3.4 semitones)**.

## Bouzouki Physics and Construction Variants
### Greek Trichordo, Greek Tetrachordo, and Irish Flat-Back Constructions
Three distinct instrument architectures share the bouzouki name, differing in body geometry, scale length, course count, and string-pairing strategy. According to luthier specifications and specialist vendor guides, both the traditional three-course Greek *trichordo* and the mid-20th-century four-course Greek *tetrachordo* utilize a deep, staved pear-shaped bowl back with a flat top and a long ~27-fret neck (~60 cm neck vs. ~40 cm body, with scale lengths typically around 680–690 mm). By contrast, the **Irish bouzouki** (developed in the 1960s–1970s via Johnny Moynihan, luthier Peter Abnett, and Dónal Lunny) employs a wider guitar-like body with flat sides and a flat or slightly arched three-piece back, ~24 frets, and typically shorter scale lengths (~610–635 mm, or 24–25 in, though variants span ~530 mm to 26–27 in / 665 mm).

### Octave-Paired vs. Unison-Paired String Physics and Scale Dispersion
The sharp acoustic contrast between Greek and Irish bouzoukis hinges on **course pairing** and **scale length**. On both the Greek *trichordo* (D course) and *tetrachordo* (C and F courses), the bass courses pair a thick wound string with a thin plain string tuned an octave higher—with the thick string mounted above the thin string so downward plectrum strokes strike the low fundamental first. Because f_{\text{oct}} = 2 f_{\text{bass}}, an octave course does **not** form a degenerate symmetric/antisymmetric double-decay pair at the fundamental; instead, the thin string's fundamental excites the bridge alongside the thick string's second harmonic, while the thin string's own higher harmonics add bright metallic upper partials. Conversely, Dónal Lunny's unison conversion on the Irish bouzouki (G2/G2–D3/D3–A3/A3–D4/D4) doubles fundamental bass radiation and restores classic Weinreich/Woodhouse symmetric-antisymmetric bridge coupling across all four courses, extending sustain.
Scale length and string gauge likewise control stiff-string dispersion B across bouzouki designs. Long-scale instruments (650–680 mm) maintain low inharmonicity using thinner plain steel strings (0.011"–0.016"), whereas shorter-scale setups (~560 mm) requiring heavier gauges (such as 0.020" plain steel) raise B nearly threefold into mandolin-like territory:

### Bowl-Back Modal Spectrum vs. Flat-Back Enclosure Behavior
Peer-reviewed measurements by Brezas et al. (2023) combining Electronic Speckle Pattern Interferometry (ESPI), impulse response testing, and FEM modeling provide direct modal benchmarks for a wooden Greek bouzouki (W02) versus a carbon-fiber Greek bouzouki (C01). On wooden W02, the fundamental (1,1) soundboard mode occurs at **185 Hz** (rising to 235 Hz in stiffer carbon-fiber C01), placing it squarely within the lower open-string range (C3 = 130.81 Hz, F3 = 174.61 Hz, A[span_390](start_span)[span_390](end_span)[span_391](start_span)[span_391](end_span)3 = 220 Hz). Higher W02 modes appear at (2,2) **436 Hz** (C01: 445 Hz), (2,3) **635 Hz** (C01: 655 Hz), (3,2) **710 Hz** (C01: 705 Hz), and (4,2) **796 Hz** (C01: 820 Hz), with an experimental (1,2) cross mode observed at 450 Hz on C01.
Echoing the rigid bowl-back mandolin, the staved Greek bouzouki bowl concentrates radiation forward with a rapid, articulate attack and shorter woody tail. By contrast, although no peer-reviewed experimental modal frequency table was found in the reviewed literature for the **Irish flat-back bouzouki**, luthier descriptions and guitar-family reflex acoustics suggest that its wider flat or gently arched back allows coupled back-plate flexing (analogous to guitar-style low-frequency triplet coupling), helping support its G2 (98 Hz) bass course and sustaining chordal drones longer than a rigid bowl-back.
